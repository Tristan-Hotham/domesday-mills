Domesday Book counted England's mills in 1086. Nine hundred and forty years later, I went looking for them on today's maps. The old watermill places are still marked by mills. The textile mills of the Industrial Revolution went somewhere else.

Tealby is a village in Lincolnshire. In 1086 Domesday Book recorded {{examples.Tealby.hh|int}} households there and {{examples.Tealby.mills|f1}} mills, one of the highest counts of any place in England. Today a watermill is mapped {{examples.Tealby.d_water_m|int}} m from the centre of its Domesday grid square. Coincidence, or does a good mill site stay a mill site for nine centuries?

Mills are a lovely test of how long places last. A watermill needs a particular spot: a stream with enough fall, a weir, a channel to the wheel. Once those are dug, building again on the same spot is easier than starting somewhere new. And because Domesday recorded mills manor by manor, it gives a national "before" picture that open data can be set against.

How many mills?

The figure you usually read is 5,624 mills, and Wikipedia's watermill article adds a later estimate of 6,082. Adding up every entry in the Hull Domesday Project's coding, I get {{domesday.mills_total|f2c}}, in {{domesday.entries_with_mills|int}} entries. The quarter is not a typo: Domesday often records half a mill, or a share of one, and {{domesday.entries_fractional|int}} entries do. How you count those shares, and mills that two lords held between them, explains most of the gap between the totals.

Norfolk has the most mills ({{counties.by_name.Norfolk.mills1086|int}}), then Lincolnshire and Wiltshire. But for its size Wiltshire is the mill county: {{counties.by_name.Wiltshire.mills_per_100hh|f1}} mills for every 100 recorded households, the highest rate anywhere. Giving each entry's mills to the places it describes leaves {{sample.mill_places|int}} places with at least part of a mill, out of {{sample.places|int}} Domesday places with a location.

The old mill places still have mills

For every Domesday place I measured the distance to the nearest modern trace of a mill: buildings mapped as watermills, windmills and other mills in OpenStreetMap and Wikidata, and features named Mill on the Ordnance Survey's gazetteer. Within 1 km, {{share.1km.water.mill|pct1}} of the places that had a mill in 1086 have a mapped watermill today, against {{share.1km.water.nomill|pct1}} of the places that did not. That is {{share.1km.water.ratio|f1}} times as many.

Mill places were bigger, and some counties are mapped much better than others, so I also compared each mill place with places of similar size in the same county. Against that local yardstick the mill places have {{share.1km.water.ratio_local|f1}} times the expected rate. A regression that also allows for distance to the nearest stream and the steepness of the ground gives an odds ratio of {{models_1km.water.or_|f2}}.

The right-hand chart is my favourite part. Windmills are a later technology: the earliest certain reference to one in northern Europe dates from 1185, a century after Domesday. So if the old mill places only had more mills because they were bigger villages, or better mapped, they should have more windmills too. They do not. {{share.1km.wind.mill|pct1}} have a mapped windmill within 1 km, against {{share.1km.wind.nomill|pct1}} for other places, and after allowing for county and size the odds ratio is {{models_1km.wind.or_|f2}}.

The more mills a place had, the stronger the link. Places with three or more mills in 1086 have a watermill within 1 km {{dose.4.water1|pct1}} of the time, {{dose_ratio_water_3plus_vs_none|f1}} times the rate for places with none.

County by county the link is much weaker (a rank correlation of {{counties.spearman.traces_built.rho|f2}}). Modern traces reflect how keenly each county's mills have been mapped, and where later industry built. That is a useful warning about open data: compare like with like, inside a county, or you mostly measure the mappers.

The textile mills went somewhere else

Then the Industrial Revolution question. Did the cotton, wool, silk and flax mills reuse the old corn-mill places? Wikidata gives me {{textile.textile.n|int}} textile mills in England, and {{textile.north_share|pct0}} of them are in the North West or Yorkshire. The median one stands {{textile.textile.med_km|f1}} km from the nearest Domesday mill place. The median watermill stands {{textile.water.med_km|f1}} km away.

The main reason is that Domesday recorded almost no mills where the cotton industry grew. In the Domesday data the North West has {{region_domesday.North West.places|int}} places and just {{region_domesday.North West.mill_places|int}} with a mill. The map of the north makes the point: the brown Domesday circles stop where the purple textile mills begin.

Even comparing like with like inside the North West and Yorkshire, none of the {{textile.north.textile.n|int}} northern textile mills is within 2 km of a Domesday mill place, against {{textile.north.water.within2|pct0}} of the watermills there. And only {{textile.river100.textile|pct0}} of textile mills stand within 100 m of a watercourse on the Ordnance Survey's river network, against {{textile.river100.water|pct0}} of watermills. The factories of the Industrial Revolution were a new geography laid over the old one, and the old one carried on.

How I did this

The Domesday figures are the Hull Domesday Project's coding of every entry, via the realm-domesday repository and my Domesday Destiny study, with each place at the centre of its 1 km grid square. Modern mills come from OpenStreetMap ({{modern.osm|int}} objects) and Wikidata ({{modern.wikidata|int}} items), and Mill names from OS Open Names ({{modern.osnames|int}} features). Rivers are from OS Open Rivers and terrain from the Mapzen terrain tiles.

These are traces of mills, not a census. Open data hold the mills someone has mapped or listed, and coverage is uneven, which is why every comparison is made within counties. Distances under about 700 m are within the precision of a Domesday grid square. I cannot tell water-powered from steam-powered textile mills, and the textile list is small. And a watermill near a Domesday mill place fits continuity of the site; it does not prove it is the same mill.
